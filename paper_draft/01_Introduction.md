# 1. Introduction

Natural language processing (NLP) has advanced rapidly through the development of distributed word representations, subword modeling, neural language models, and multilingual representation learning [1]. These approaches have made it possible to transfer linguistic information across languages, support retrieval and question answering, and reduce the need for task-specific feature engineering [2]. Multilingual embeddings are especially important for languages with limited annotated data because they can connect related lexical items, represent semantic similarity, and provide a common computational space for downstream tasks [3]. However, the success of multilingual representation learning remains uneven. Languages with large digital corpora and mature linguistic resources benefit more consistently from these methods, while low-resource and morphologically rich languages often remain underrepresented in training data, benchmarks, and evaluation protocols [4].

Low-resource NLP is not only a problem of corpus size. It also involves uneven data quality, limited linguistic annotation, scarce expert-reviewed benchmarks, and insufficient modeling of language-specific structure [5]. Generic multilingual models may encode some cross-lingual regularities through shared subwords or distributional similarity, but they do not necessarily represent the linguistic relations that are most informative for a particular language family [6]. For related languages, important evidence may be found in cognates, shared roots, regular sound correspondences, inflectional and derivational morphology, and historically inherited lexical material [7]. When these signals are not represented explicitly, multilingual embeddings may capture surface similarity while missing deeper genealogical or morphological relationships [8].

The Turkic language family provides a useful setting for studying this problem. Turkic languages are geographically widespread and linguistically diverse, including languages such as Turkish, Uzbek, Uyghur, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Turkmen, Azerbaijani, and historically attested varieties such as Old Turkic [9]. These languages share historical origins and many lexical correspondences, while also differing in orthography, phonology, morphology, contact history, and modern standardization [10]. The family is also characterized by rich agglutinative morphology, where roots combine with sequences of suffixes to express grammatical and derivational meaning [11]. This morphological structure creates challenges for sparse lexical representations because a single lemma may appear in many inflected or derived forms [12]. At the same time, the regularity of Turkic morphology offers a source of structured information that can support lexical normalization, cross-language comparison, and semantic representation [13].

Cross-language lexical relationships are another central property of the Turkic family. Cognates and historically related forms can link lexical items across languages even when contemporary forms differ because of sound change, borrowing, orthographic variation, or semantic shift [14]. For example, cognate groups may reveal relationships that are not obvious from surface string similarity alone. Historical forms can also provide intermediate evidence for lexical continuity and divergence across the family [15]. These properties make Turkic languages a suitable test case for exploring whether multilingual embeddings can be improved by incorporating cognate, morphological, and historical linguistic knowledge in a coordinated representation framework [16].

Despite this potential, computational resources for Turkic NLP remain limited compared with high-resource languages [17]. Existing resources are often fragmented by language, task, or data type. Some languages have stronger web presence and NLP tooling, while others have fewer curated datasets and fewer reusable benchmarks [18]. In addition, many representation learning approaches for Turkic languages rely primarily on general subword modeling or monolingual corpora and do not explicitly incorporate cognate structure or historical linguistic evidence [19]. Morphology-aware approaches exist in broader NLP research, but multilingual representations for related agglutinative languages often lack a unified design that connects roots, lemmas, suffix patterns, cognate sets, and historical forms [20]. As a result, current embeddings may be useful for basic similarity and retrieval, but they may not fully exploit the linguistic organization of the Turkic family [21].

The problem addressed in this paper is therefore the lack of a reproducible, linguistically informed embedding framework for Turkic languages that integrates three complementary sources of evidence: cognate relationships, morphological structure, and historical linguistic knowledge. A cognate-aware embedding model should not only learn that words occur in similar contexts, but also represent cross-language lexical relationships that reflect shared origin or systematic correspondence [22]. A morphology-aware multilingual representation should account for the way Turkic words are built from roots, lemmas, suffixes, and derivational patterns [23]. A historically informed representation should make use of diachronic evidence where available, especially for linking modern forms to older lexical material and for interpreting language-family relationships [24].

The motivation for combining these signals is linguistic as well as computational. Cognates matter because they provide explicit cross-language anchors. In a low-resource multilingual setting, such anchors can support alignment between languages even when parallel corpora are unavailable or limited [25]. Morphology matters because Turkic languages generate many surface forms from a smaller set of lexical and grammatical units. Representations that ignore this structure may treat related forms as unrelated tokens, increasing sparsity and reducing interpretability [26]. Historical lineage matters because language-family relationships are not always visible from contemporary surface forms alone. Historical data can help explain divergence, preserve links between related forms, and provide additional structure for multilingual comparison [27]. Turkic languages are suitable for evaluating this hypothesis because they combine shared genealogical structure with substantial modern diversity, allowing a model to test whether family-specific linguistic knowledge can support representation learning without assuming that all languages are identical [28].

This work is developed within the TurkicGrammarAI research repository. The repository contains several interacting layers that support the proposed study. The morphology layer provides rule-based and lexical resources for roots, lemmas, suffixes, and language-specific analyses. The cognate layer represents cross-language cognate groups and alignment-oriented resources. The historical layer stores historical forms and links relevant to diachronic comparison. The embedding layer uses these resources to support multilingual representation, semantic search, retrieval-augmented evidence selection, and question answering workflows. In this paper, these layers are treated as a research infrastructure for investigating linguistically informed embeddings rather than as a claim of complete linguistic coverage or production-level readiness.

The main contributions of this work are:

1. A cognate-aware multilingual embedding framework for Turkic languages that integrates lexical, morphological, cognate, and historical linguistic resources within a unified research pipeline.

2. A structured representation of Turkic linguistic evidence covering multiple modern Turkic languages and Old Turkic, with explicit attention to roots, lemmas, suffix patterns, cognate relationships, and historical forms.

3. A reproducible evaluation design that connects embedding quality with morphology evaluation, cognate evaluation, semantic search, retrieval-augmented evidence selection, and question answering, while distinguishing internal benchmark evidence from expert-reviewed validation.

4. An ablation-oriented research plan for examining the individual roles of cognate, morphology, and historical features in multilingual embedding behavior.

5. A repository-supported reproducibility package that documents figures, tables, analysis files, validation reports, dataset manifests, and publication-facing artifacts for reviewer inspection.

The scope of these contributions is intentionally limited to repository-supported evidence. The paper does not claim that the current resources solve Turkic NLP, provide complete expert-reviewed gold standards, or establish state-of-the-art performance. Instead, it presents a transparent framework for studying how language-family knowledge can be incorporated into multilingual embeddings for Turkic languages, and how such a framework can be evaluated through documented and reproducible research artifacts.

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work on multilingual embeddings, low-resource NLP, morphology-aware representation learning, cognate modeling, and Turkic language resources. Section 3 describes the materials and methods, including the linguistic resources, data organization, and cognate-aware embedding framework. Section 4 presents the experimental setup, evaluation tasks, baselines, metrics, and reproducibility protocol. Section 5 reports and discusses the evaluation results across embedding, cognate, retrieval, and question answering tasks. Section 6 presents ablation analysis and error analysis to examine the contribution of individual linguistic knowledge sources. Section 7 discusses threats to validity, including dataset provenance, benchmark leakage risk, expert-review limitations, and generalization beyond the current language set. Section 8 concludes the paper and outlines future work toward expert-reviewed gold datasets, independent benchmarks, and broader linguistically informed multilingual representation learning.
