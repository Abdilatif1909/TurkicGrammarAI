# 2. Related Work

## 2.1 Distributional Word Embeddings

Distributional word embeddings represent lexical meaning through contextual co-occurrence patterns [29]. Neural embedding methods such as Word2Vec replaced sparse lexical representations with dense vectors that can support similarity estimation, clustering, retrieval, and downstream prediction tasks. The continuous bag-of-words and skip-gram objectives made it practical to train reusable word representations from large text collections, and these models became a foundation for many later neural NLP systems [31]. However, word-level embeddings depend heavily on corpus size and token frequency. They represent each surface form as a separate unit, which creates sparsity for rare forms and prevents direct representation of unseen words.

FastText and related subword embedding methods address this limitation by representing words through character n-grams or other internal units [33]. This design is important for morphologically rich languages because it allows the model to construct vectors for forms that may not occur frequently in the training data. Subword embeddings are therefore relevant to agglutinative languages, where roots combine with suffix sequences to produce many surface forms [35]. Nevertheless, subword modeling is not the same as linguistic modeling. Character n-grams can capture useful surface regularities, but they do not explicitly distinguish roots, derivational suffixes, inflectional markers, cognate relations, or historical correspondences.

Multilingual embeddings extend distributional learning by making lexical representations comparable across languages [37]. They are useful for bilingual lexicon induction, cross-language retrieval, machine translation support, multilingual classification, and low-resource transfer. In high-resource settings, multilingual embeddings can be trained or aligned using parallel corpora, bilingual dictionaries, comparable corpora, or shared multilingual objectives [39]. In low-resource settings, these resources are often limited, noisy, or unevenly distributed across languages. As a result, multilingual embedding quality may vary substantially between languages, especially when some languages dominate the available training data [41].

## 2.2 Cross-Lingual and Multilingual Representation Learning

Cross-lingual representation learning aims to connect lexical or contextual representations across languages [42]. One common strategy is to train monolingual embeddings separately and then learn a mapping between vector spaces using bilingual supervision. Such alignment methods can be effective when seed dictionaries or parallel data are reliable, but performance may degrade when supervision is small, noisy, or domain-specific [44]. Unsupervised alignment methods reduce the need for bilingual resources, yet they often assume structural similarity between embedding spaces and may be unstable for low-resource or typologically different languages.

Multilingual pretraining provides another approach by training a single model over many languages with shared vocabularies and self-supervised objectives [46]. These models have improved transfer learning and enabled zero-shot or few-shot applications for many languages. However, multilingual pretraining does not remove resource imbalance. Languages with larger corpora, more standardized orthographies, and stronger web presence often receive better representation, while smaller languages may be underrepresented in vocabulary construction and parameter learning [48]. This is especially relevant for language families whose members differ widely in available digital resources.

For related languages, transfer learning can benefit from shared linguistic structure [50]. Cognates, shared roots, similar morphological processes, and historical correspondences can provide evidence for cross-language alignment even when parallel corpora are unavailable. Standard multilingual embeddings may capture some of these signals indirectly, but they usually do not separate distributional similarity from cognacy, morphology, or historical lineage [52]. This limits interpretability and may reduce their usefulness for linguistic search, language-family comparison, and historically informed retrieval.

## 2.3 Morphology-Aware Embeddings

Morphology-aware embeddings incorporate internal word structure into representation learning [54]. Relevant features include roots, stems, lemmas, affixes, part-of-speech information, inflectional categories, derivational patterns, and segmentation boundaries. Such information is important for morphologically rich languages because a single lemma may produce many inflected or derived forms, many of which occur rarely in available corpora [56]. If these forms are treated as unrelated tokens, distributional models may fail to connect words that are linguistically related.

Approaches to morphology-aware representation learning differ in how explicitly they use linguistic analysis [58]. Some methods rely on character-level or subword-level modeling as an implicit approximation of morphology. Others inject morphological information directly through analyzers, lexicons, tags, morpheme embeddings, or augmented training data [60]. Direct feature injection can improve interpretability because it makes roots, suffixes, and grammatical categories visible to the model or to the evaluation procedure. However, such approaches require reliable linguistic resources, which are often incomplete or inconsistent for low-resource languages [62].

Agglutinative languages make morphology-aware modeling particularly important. In these languages, grammatical information is often expressed through ordered suffix chains, and surface word forms may be long and sparse. A purely word-level representation may fragment related forms, while a purely character-based method may confuse productive morphology with accidental string overlap [64]. A more linguistically grounded approach should preserve language-specific morphology while also allowing comparison across related languages. For Turkic languages, this means using roots, lemmas, suffix patterns, and derivational structures as structured evidence rather than treating them only as surface character sequences [66].

## 2.4 Cognate-Based and Historical Linguistic NLP

Cognates are lexical items in related languages that descend from a common historical source [67]. In NLP, cognate information has been used for bilingual lexicon induction, low-resource transfer, language comparison, and historical reconstruction. Cognate detection may rely on orthographic similarity, phonetic similarity, edit distance, sound correspondence patterns, semantic similarity, or supervised classification [69]. More recent approaches combine neural representations with linguistic features, but cognate modeling remains difficult when scripts, phonology, and meanings diverge across languages.

Cognate resources are valuable because they provide explicit cross-language anchors [71]. Unlike ordinary translation pairs, cognates encode historical relatedness and may connect several languages within a family. They can support multilingual representation learning by linking lexical items that share origin, even when distributional evidence is limited. However, cognacy is not identical to synonymy. Cognates may undergo semantic shift, may coexist with borrowings, and may be confused with false friends [73]. A cognate-aware embedding framework must therefore treat cognates as linguistic evidence that requires validation and contextual interpretation.

Historical linguistics adds another layer of evidence by describing how languages change over time [75]. Historical forms, sound correspondences, and diachronic lexical relationships can help explain why modern forms differ and how related words developed. Computational historical linguistics has addressed cognate detection, language phylogeny, diachronic semantic change, and etymological modeling [77]. Yet historical knowledge is not often integrated into practical multilingual embedding systems. Such integration is challenging because historical data may be sparse, uncertain, unevenly documented, or represented through different scripts and transcription conventions. For this reason, historically informed embeddings must document provenance and distinguish attested forms, reconstructed forms, and projected links [79].

## 2.5 Turkic Language NLP Resources

Turkic NLP requires attention to both shared family structure and language-specific diversity. Turkic languages are written in different scripts, used across different regions, and represented unevenly in digital corpora [81]. Some languages have stronger NLP resources, while others have fewer annotated datasets, fewer reusable benchmarks, and less standardized computational tooling. This imbalance complicates multilingual modeling because better-resourced languages may dominate training and evaluation, while lower-resource languages receive weaker representations [83].

Morphological analysis is central for Turkic NLP because Turkic languages are widely described as agglutinative and suffixing [84]. Computational resources may include lexicons, morphology rules, suffix inventories, lemma dictionaries, derivational rules, and analyzers. These resources support lemmatization, morphological parsing, search, generation, and evaluation [86]. However, resources are often developed separately for individual languages and may differ in format, coverage, and linguistic assumptions. A multilingual embedding framework therefore requires resource normalization and careful documentation of language coverage [88].

Lexical and historical resources are also uneven across Turkic languages [89]. Word lists, cognate sets, historical forms, and benchmark files may be curated, generated, projected, or normalized from available sources. This distinction is important for scientific evaluation. Internally generated benchmarks are useful for development and regression testing, but stronger claims require independent and expert-reviewed validation [91]. A reproducible Turkic NLP study should therefore document dataset provenance, validation status, benchmark construction, and known limitations.

## 2.6 Research Gap

Existing work has developed distributional embeddings, multilingual alignment, morphology-aware modeling, cognate detection, and historical linguistic NLP as important but often separate research areas [93]. Standard word embeddings and multilingual models emphasize distributional learning and transfer, but they rarely encode cognate relations, morphology rules, and historical lineage as explicit structured evidence. Morphology-aware models address internal word structure, but they are frequently designed for single languages or for general feature augmentation rather than for language-family-aware multilingual representation [95]. Cognate and historical methods provide cross-language linguistic evidence, but they are often evaluated as separate tasks instead of being integrated into embedding construction, retrieval, and question answering workflows.

This separation leaves a clear gap for Turkic languages. The family combines shared historical origins, rich agglutinative morphology, cross-language lexical relationships, and low-resource constraints [97]. These properties suggest that a representation framework should combine distributional evidence with structured linguistic knowledge. However, morphology, cognates, and historical lineage have rarely been integrated into a single reproducible embedding framework for multiple Turkic languages [98]. Without such integration, it is difficult to evaluate whether these knowledge sources complement each other or how they affect downstream tasks such as semantic search, retrieval-augmented evidence selection, and question answering.

The proposed approach is motivated by this gap. It treats cognate groups, morphology resources, and historical forms as structured evidence for multilingual Turkic embeddings rather than as isolated post hoc analyses [100]. It also emphasizes reproducibility by linking representation learning to documented datasets, evaluation files, validation reports, and reviewer-facing artifacts. This framing supports a cautious and auditable study of linguistically informed multilingual embeddings for Turkic languages without relying on unsupported claims about completeness or general performance [102].
