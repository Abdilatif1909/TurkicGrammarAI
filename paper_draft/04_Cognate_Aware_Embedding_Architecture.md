# 4. Cognate-Aware Embedding Architecture

## 4.1 System Overview

The proposed architecture organizes the available Turkic lexical resources as a sequence of linguistically informed transformations rather than training directly on unannotated word lists. Its principal stages are morphology, cognate alignment, historical-form association, embedding-dataset construction, FastText training, semantic search, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) retrieval, and question answering (QA). Figure 1 presents the central representation-learning path from the lexical and linguistic resources through feature injection to the resulting embedding space. The downstream search, retrieval, and QA services consume this shared representation and its associated metadata.

The first stage assigns lexical and morphological structure to surface forms. Each usable record is represented by a surface form, lemma, root, language identifier, and a list of normalized morphological features. Cognate processing then links forms that belong to the same repository cognate group. Historical processing associates a form with an ordered lineage containing available Proto Turkic, Old Turkic, intermediate, and modern-language forms. These three evidence types are merged into a JSONL embedding dataset. The dataset is subsequently converted into a line-oriented training corpus in which linguistic attributes are expressed as tokens occurring beside lexical forms.

The resulting corpus is used to train a FastText skip-gram model. FastText provides word vectors while also modeling character-level subword information, which is relevant to a multilingual collection containing productive morphology, spelling variation, and several scripts. After training, the dataset is compacted into a semantic index with lookup maps for words, lemmas, language-specific roots, cognate groups, features, and historical forms. Semantic search uses the trained vectors together with these maps. The RAG retriever combines semantic evidence with cognate, historical, morphological, and dictionary-source evidence, while the QA layer selects a query term, retrieves supporting records, classifies the question type, and constructs a structured answer from those records. Thus, the same enriched lexical representation supports both intrinsic embedding use and downstream evidence retrieval.

The architecture is deliberately modular at the data-processing level. Morphology, cognate membership, and lineage are preserved as explicit fields before being transformed into corpus signals. This makes the linguistic basis of a training line inspectable and allows the semantic index to retain the original evidence after vector training. The architecture does not require a separate neural module for every linguistic feature; instead, it exposes repository annotations to FastText through controlled co-occurrence and retains metadata for downstream candidate expansion.

## 4.2 Morphological Enrichment

Morphological enrichment is based on four related types of information: lemma, root, normalized morphological features, and derivational information. The lemma provides a citation-like lexical identity for a surface form. The root represents the base used to connect morphologically related forms within a language. When a source does not provide these fields, dataset construction applies repository-defined fallbacks: the surface form is used as the lemma, and the lemma or surface form is used as the root. These fallbacks ensure schema completeness, although they do not imply that every fallback value is an independently validated morphological analysis.

The feature field stores normalized labels derived from resource metadata, morphology benchmarks, part-of-speech categories, and suffix-based rules implemented by the dataset builder. The constructed dataset contains 11 observed feature labels: `NOUN`, `VERB`, `ADJECTIVE`, `PLURAL`, `POSS_1PL`, `DATIVE`, `ACCUSATIVE`, `LOCATIVE`, `ABLATIVE`, `NEGATIVE`, and `DERIVATIONAL`. The wider feature encoder defines a larger universal inventory, but Section 4 describes the labels actually reported for the 100,000-record training dataset. Derivational information is represented through the `DERIVATIONAL` feature and through roots and derived surface forms. Repository derivational resources additionally distinguish such patterns as noun-to-profession, verb-to-agent, verb-to-noun, noun-to-adjective, and adjective-to-noun formations.

Morphological information is injected into the FastText corpus as textual context. For each dataset record, the corpus-preparation stage emits the surface form and lemma directly, converts the root into a `ROOT_<root>` token, and converts each feature into a `FEATURE_<feature>` token. A language marker of the form `LANG_<language>` is included in the same line. Consequently, a surface form is trained in a local context that identifies its lexical base, language, and available grammatical properties. The injection is deterministic: token values are normalized by trimming whitespace and replacing internal spaces with underscores.

The corpus also contains grouped morphology signal lines. Records are grouped by language and root, and a line beginning with the corresponding `ROOT_<root>` marker is emitted when at least two distinct surface forms share that root. Up to 16 forms are included in such a line. Feature groups are formed across records in a similar manner: a `FEATURE_<feature>` marker is followed by up to 32 distinct forms carrying that feature. These additional lines make the relationship among morphological variants explicit rather than relying only on their occurrence in separate record lines. They also preserve a distinction between language-specific root clusters and feature-level groupings that may span languages.

## 4.3 Cognate-Aware Representation

Cognate membership is encoded through tokens of the form `COGNATE_<id>`. Repository cognate identifiers use values such as `cog_000001`; during corpus construction, the `cog_` prefix is removed and the remaining identifier is uppercased, producing a marker such as `COGNATE_000001`. Records without an assigned cognate group receive the explicit marker `COGNATE_NONE`. This design gives the training process a stable group-level symbol while retaining the original cognate identifier in the JSONL dataset and semantic index.

The embedding dataset contains 23,018 records assigned to 2,000 unique cognate groups. A cognate-derived record stores the language-specific form, its language code, the group identifier, and the lineage associated with that group. Cognate membership is not represented only as an isolated field. In every record-level training line, the surface form occurs beside its cognate marker. The corpus builder also collects all records sharing a non-empty cognate group and emits two directional signal lines. One line places up to 24 distinct forms before the cognate and lineage anchors; the second places the anchors before the forms. These paired lines expose the same group relation in both local orders within the fixed FastText context window.

Cross-language alignment therefore arises from shared co-occurrence rather than from a separate alignment network. Forms written in different languages or scripts are placed in the context of the same `COGNATE_<id>` marker. The cognate line can also include the lineage-stage and lineage-form markers associated with the group. This establishes a common training context for forms that may have limited character overlap. The representation remains group-aware rather than pair-specific: all available members of a cognate set share the same anchor, enabling one group token to connect several language-specific forms.

The downstream index preserves this structure through a `by_cognate` map. When a query exactly matches an indexed word, semantic search can expand the candidate set to other records in the same cognate group and label them as cognate and cross-language candidates. Cognate information thus contributes at two points: it shapes co-occurrence during embedding training and provides an explicit candidate-expansion relation during retrieval.

## 4.4 Historical Lineage Encoding

Historical information is encoded with two complementary token families. `LINEAGE_<stage>` identifies a historical or language stage, while `LINEAGE_FORM_<form>` identifies an attested or repository-specified form associated with that stage. Stage names are uppercased and spaces are replaced with underscores. For example, Proto Turkic and Old Turkic are represented as `LINEAGE_PROTO_TURKIC` and `LINEAGE_OLD_TURKIC`. A corresponding reconstructed or historical form is emitted as a `LINEAGE_FORM_<form>` token.

The repository constructs lineage in two ways. Cognate groups are associated with a Proto Turkic form and an ordered set of available forms for Old Turkic, Uyghur, Uzbek, Turkish, Azerbaijani, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, and Turkmen. Separate historical-form records contain Proto Turkic, Old Turkic, Middle Turkic, and a selected modern-language form. The embedding dataset contains 25,018 records with historical lineage and 215,162 lineage entries. These entries provide the architecture with links among reconstructed stages, historical records, and modern Turkic forms, while their scientific status remains that of repository metadata requiring appropriate provenance and expert review.

At corpus-construction time, all available stage and form markers are appended to the record line. For cognate groups, the same lineage markers are also used as anchors in grouped cognate signal lines. This creates an explicit co-occurrence path from a modern form to its group, historical stages, and lineage forms. The encoding does not calculate historical sound change or infer a phylogenetic tree during FastText training. It converts already available lineage relations into observable context tokens.

Historical metadata is also retained in the semantic index. Each record stores its complete lineage, and a `by_lineage_form` map associates every lineage form with the records that contain it. A query matching a historical form can therefore retrieve related records even when the queried form is not the primary modern word field. If those records also have a cognate identifier, retrieval can continue from the historical match to the wider cognate group.

## 4.5 Embedding Dataset Construction

The embedding dataset is stored as `embedding_dataset.jsonl` and contains exactly 100,000 records. It covers Uzbek, Turkish, Azerbaijani, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Turkmen, Uyghur, and Old Turkic. The language distribution is 18,314 Uzbek records, 15,798 Turkish, 10,372 Azerbaijani, 10,582 Kazakh, 10,499 Kyrgyz, 10,102 Turkmen, 13,916 Uyghur, and 10,417 Old Turkic. The resource contains 88,712 unique surface forms, 26,797 unique lemmas, and 26,798 unique roots. Morphological features are present in 75,429 records.

Each record contains `surface_form`, `lemma`, `root`, `language`, `features`, `cognate_group`, `historical_lineage`, and `source`. The records are assembled from cross-language cognates, morphology benchmarks, historical forms, corpus hooks where available, language word datasets, and lemma dictionaries. The reported source distribution includes 70,170 word-dataset records, 15,948 cognate records, 8,611 lemma-dictionary records, 2,000 historical-form records, and smaller morphology-benchmark contributions. During construction, records are deduplicated by language, surface form, lemma, root, and source; 2,282 duplicate records were skipped in the reported build.

The builder processes source streams in a fixed order and stops when the 100,000-record target is reached. This ordering is part of the reproducible repository procedure and means that the final JSONL file is an embedding-oriented integration of resources, not a simple copy of the raw word collection. Missing lemma or root values are filled by the schema fallbacks described above, feature labels are normalized, and cognate lineage is attached when a word matches a cognate form. The metadata remains available after corpus generation so that retrieval can use the same linguistic relations independently of vector similarity.

## 4.6 FastText Training Strategy

The model is trained with the Gensim implementation of FastText using the skip-gram objective (`sg=1`). As reported in Table 3, the vector size is 300, the context window is 5, the minimum token count is 1, and training is performed for 20 epochs with 12 workers. The resulting vocabulary contains 143,011 entries. Table 3 also records the corpus, model, and exported vector paths and the observed training time for the repository run.

Training uses the line-oriented corpus produced from the JSONL records and grouped signal lines. Each ordinary line contains a surface form, lemma, root marker, language marker, cognate marker, zero or more feature markers, and zero or more lineage-stage and lineage-form markers. Additional lines explicitly group cognate forms, root-related forms, and forms sharing a morphological feature. The model vocabulary is built from this complete corpus before the 20 training epochs are executed.

FastText’s subword modeling is used as implemented by the library; the repository training function does not override character n-gram parameters. Accordingly, this architecture relies on FastText defaults for subword configuration rather than claiming separately tuned n-gram lengths. The subword mechanism complements the injected linguistic markers: character n-grams model internal form similarity and permit representations for uncommon strings, while group markers provide explicit contexts for relations that may not be recoverable from orthographic overlap alone.

The minimum count of one retains low-frequency lexical, historical, and metadata tokens. This is consistent with the architecture’s need to preserve specific group and lineage markers, although it also increases the vocabulary. The five-token window provides local interaction among the fields in record lines and among forms and anchors in grouped lines. The trained model and word2vec-format vectors are saved as repository artifacts for loading by the semantic-search service.

## 4.7 Semantic Retrieval Integration

The downstream retrieval stack uses both vectors and preserved metadata. A semantic index is built from the 100,000-record JSONL dataset and stores compact records together with maps by word, lemma, language-specific root, cognate group, feature, and lineage form. Semantic search begins with exact word or historical-form matches, expands candidates through lemma, root, cognate, and lineage relations, and scores candidates using FastText cosine similarity plus fixed metadata-dependent boosts. Results are deduplicated by word and language.

The RAG retriever extends this process by combining semantic, cognate, historical, morphology, and dictionary-source components in a manually weighted relevance score. It returns the lexical form, lemma, root, language, cognate group, lineage, component scores, source type, internal source identifier, confidence, and source trace. This layer should therefore be understood as metadata-enhanced retrieval for RAG use, rather than as an additional learned reranker.

The QA service operates on retrieved evidence. It applies rule-based question-type detection for historical, cross-language, morphology, and cognate questions; selects a query term by testing candidate content words; and constructs a template-based answer from the highest-ranked documents. It also returns supporting records and internal evidence traces. The QA component is consequently a retrieval-based structured answer builder, not a generative language model. This high-level integration completes the pipeline from enriched embeddings to inspectable lexical answers.

## 4.8 Design Rationale

Morphology is included because Turkic languages are productively suffixing, and surface forms alone can divide evidence belonging to the same lexical base. Lemma, root, and feature markers provide explicit contexts for related forms while preserving language-specific root groupings. Derivational labels additionally distinguish lexical formation from purely inflectional variation when the repository supplies that information.

Cognates are included because orthographic or subword similarity is not sufficient for all related forms across languages and scripts. A shared `COGNATE_<id>` anchor introduces a direct group-level relation and supports cross-language candidate expansion after training. Historical lineage is included to connect modern forms to available Proto Turkic, Old Turkic, Middle Turkic, and modern-language stages. The design treats these links as structured metadata and does not infer unsupported historical relations.

FastText is used because its subword representation is compatible with morphologically complex word forms and can complement explicit linguistic tokens within a comparatively simple training procedure. It also produces directly usable word vectors for cosine-based search and supports loading in the repository service without an additional transformer inference layer. The combined design separates two roles: FastText supplies distributional and subword similarity, while retained morphology, cognate, and lineage metadata supplies explicit linguistic relations and traceable retrieval paths.
