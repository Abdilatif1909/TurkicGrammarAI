# 6. Results and Discussion

## 6.1 Embedding Quality Results

Table 4 summarizes the intrinsic evaluation of the cognate-aware FastText model on the 5,000-pair embedding-quality benchmark. The model obtained Top-1, Top-5, and Top-10 accuracies of 40.80%, 73.66%, and 86.06%, respectively. The difference between Top-1 and Top-5 indicates that the expected related form was frequently present among the leading candidates even when it was not the nearest candidate. The further increase to Top-10 shows that most positive targets were recovered within a moderately sized neighborhood. However, the Top-1 result also shows that the first neighbor was not the expected target for a majority of positive benchmark pairs. The embedding space therefore supports broad candidate retrieval more strongly than exact first-rank selection.

Table 5 provides Wilson 95% confidence intervals derived from the stored proportions and benchmark sizes. The reported intervals are 39.45–42.17% for Top-1, 72.42–74.86% for Top-5, and 85.07–86.99% for Top-10. These intervals quantify uncertainty within the internal benchmark under the assumption that benchmark items behave as independent samples. They are derived manuscript analyses rather than outputs of the original evaluator, and the benchmark’s synthetic and semi-synthetic construction limits their external interpretation.

The mean cosine similarity across all evaluated pairs was 0.577844. Positive pairs had a mean similarity of 0.595108, compared with 0.472318 for negative pairs, producing a mean separation margin of 0.122790. This direction is consistent with the intended representation: repository-defined related pairs were, on average, closer than sampled negative pairs. The margin is nevertheless an aggregate difference, not evidence of complete class separation. The repository does not store the full positive and negative similarity distributions or their standard deviations; consequently, no standardized effect size or significance test is available. Table 4 therefore reports the observed means and margin without an inferential comparison.

The absence of baseline results is a central restriction on interpretation. Table 4 explicitly records the baseline and improvement columns as “Not reported” and “Not computable.” The repository contains no separately evaluated vanilla FastText model trained without morphology, cognate, and lineage signals. It is thus possible to characterize the cognate-aware model’s absolute internal performance, but not to claim a measured improvement over standard FastText or another multilingual embedding method.

## 6.2 Dataset and Coverage Analysis

Figure 2 shows the record distribution across the eight represented language varieties. Uzbek has the largest share with 18,314 records, followed by Turkish with 15,798 and Uyghur with 13,916. Azerbaijani, Kazakh, Kyrgyz, Turkmen, and Old Turkic each contribute approximately ten thousand records: 10,372, 10,582, 10,499, 10,102, and 10,417, respectively. Figure 5 expresses the same distribution as percentage coverage. Uzbek accounts for 18.3%, Turkish for 15.8%, Uyghur for 13.9%, Kazakh for 10.6%, Kyrgyz for 10.5%, Azerbaijani and Old Turkic for 10.4% each, and Turkmen for 10.1%.

The coverage is therefore multilingual without being strictly balanced. The largest language share is below one fifth of the dataset, and five languages fall within the narrow range of 10.1–10.6%. This prevents any single language from numerically dominating the complete resource. At the same time, Uzbek contains approximately 1.8 times as many records as Turkmen, and the larger Uzbek, Turkish, and Uyghur components can influence vocabulary formation and neighborhood density.

The composition is also unequal at the lexical abstraction level. The dataset contains 88,712 unique surface forms but only 26,797 unique lemmas and 26,798 unique roots. This difference reflects the morphology-oriented construction, in which multiple surface variants may be associated with a smaller set of lexical bases. Morphological feature lists occur in 75,429 records, while 23,018 records have cognate-group assignments and 25,018 have historical lineage. The model is consequently exposed to morphology more broadly than to explicit cognate or historical metadata.

Source composition provides a further qualification. Of the 100,000 embedding records, 70,170 come from word datasets, 15,948 from cognate resources, 8,611 from lemma dictionaries, and 2,000 from historical-form records; the remainder are contributed by morphology benchmarks. This structure gives the model broad lexical coverage while reserving smaller, targeted subsets for explicit linguistic relations. It also creates heterogeneous evidence quality because raw words, generated forms, benchmark records, and historical metadata are merged into one training resource.

The presence of Uyghur and Old Turkic is relevant for script and diachronic coverage, but record counts alone do not establish equivalent normalization quality. Repository audits note that Uyghur has no normalized clean file corresponding to those available for several other languages and that some Uyghur material is projection-based. Old Turkic and Proto-Turkic-style metadata also require specialist provenance review. Figures 2 and 5 should therefore be interpreted as coverage distributions, not as evidence that all languages have equal lexical quality, normalization maturity, or external validation.

## 6.3 Cognate-Aware Representation Analysis

Figure 3 places the principal stored evaluation results in a common descriptive view: embedding Top-1, Top-5, and Top-10, semantic-search Recall@10, RAG Recall@10, and QA answer accuracy. The values are 40.8%, 73.7%, 86.1%, 82.3%, 84.3%, and 83.8%, respectively, with display values rounded in the figure. The figure is useful for summarizing the pipeline, but the bars represent different tasks, benchmark sizes, and metric definitions. They should not be treated as direct model-to-model comparisons.

Figure 4 focuses on the intrinsic positive-negative relation. The exact stored means are 0.595108 for positive pairs and 0.472318 for negative pairs, with a margin of 0.122790. This result indicates that the combination of subword modeling and explicit training markers retains useful information about benchmark-defined relations at the pair level. The positive category includes cognates, cross-language equivalents, morphological variants, and historical relations; the mean therefore aggregates several kinds of linguistic relatedness rather than measuring cognacy alone.

Cluster-level results complicate a simple interpretation of Figure 4. Across 2,000 cognate clusters, mean intra-cluster similarity was 0.634245 and mean inter-cluster similarity was 0.697581, yielding negative separation of -0.063336. The four language-family clusters likewise had intra-cluster similarity of 0.556224 and inter-cluster similarity of 0.610933, for separation of -0.054709. By contrast, the 11 morphological clusters had intra-cluster similarity of 0.699947 and inter-cluster similarity of 0.543781, producing positive separation of 0.156166.

These results show that pair-level positive separation does not imply globally clean cognate or language-family clusters. Morphological regularities appear more consistently organized under the stored cluster calculation, plausibly because repeated stems, suffixes, and character n-grams provide strong surface signals. Cognate groups can contain forms across scripts and substantial phonological divergence, while unrelated groups may still share character patterns or frequent morphology. In addition, group quality depends on the repository cognate assignments. The negative cluster separations therefore require cautious claims: the model represents some related pairs effectively, but the raw embedding geometry does not uniformly partition all cognate sets or language families.

Cross-language behavior is also weaker than exact or historical metadata lookup. In semantic search, cross-language Recall@10 was 54.57%, compared with 82.36% for cognate queries, 75.50% for morphological queries, and 100% for both exact and historical queries. The error analysis identifies cross-language ranking as a primary failure surface. High-similarity inflectional or generated variants can occupy leading positions and displace the expected form from another language.

Table 6 provides feature-contribution proxies rather than controlled ablation. Root-related evidence is available in 75,429 records and appears in 17,495 RAG results with 915 successful hits. Cognate evidence is available in 23,018 records and contributes to 7,247 results with 675 successful hits. Lineage evidence is available in 25,018 records and contributes to 9,000 results with 675 successful hits. Language markers are available for all records, with the table associating them with 17,794 results and 798 successful hits. These counts establish that each path participates in retrieval, but they do not measure the performance change caused by removing that path. No feature-removal retraining experiments are stored.

## 6.4 Semantic Search Evaluation

Semantic search was evaluated on 2,000 queries and achieved Recall@1 of 36.25%, Recall@5 of 72.95%, Recall@10 of 82.30%, and MRR of 0.529888. The progression across cutoffs resembles the intrinsic ranking pattern: acceptable results are often retrieved within the first ten positions, but substantially fewer occur at rank one. Table 5 reports derived Wilson intervals of 34.17–38.38% for Recall@1, 70.96–74.85% for Recall@5, and 80.57–83.91% for Recall@10.

Category results reveal substantial variation. Exact and historical queries each obtained Recall@10 of 100%, cognate queries obtained 82.36%, morphological queries 75.50%, and cross-language queries 54.57%. The perfect exact result is expected from direct word indexing. Historical retrieval also benefits from the explicit lineage-form map, which provides a deterministic metadata path rather than relying solely on vector proximity. These outcomes demonstrate that the semantic-search service is a hybrid vector and metadata system.

Table 7 summarizes category-level dispersion. Across the five semantic categories, mean Recall@10 was 82.49%, the minimum was 54.57%, the maximum was 100%, and the population standard deviation was 16.98. The wide range reinforces the importance of category reporting: the aggregate 82.30% result conceals much weaker cross-language behavior. The stored failure sample contains 84 cognate and 16 morphological examples, but only the first 100 failures are retained, so those counts are an error sample rather than a complete failure distribution.

## 6.5 RAG Retrieval Evaluation

The RAG retriever achieved Recall@1 of 27.00%, Recall@5 of 65.90%, Recall@10 of 84.30%, and MRR of 0.455756 on 1,000 queries. Table 5 gives derived Wilson intervals of 24.34–29.84%, 62.91–68.77%, and 81.91–86.42% for the three recall cutoffs. Compared descriptively with semantic search, RAG has lower Recall@1, Recall@5, and MRR, but slightly higher Recall@10. Because the benchmarks differ in size and category composition, this difference is not a controlled comparison.

The balanced RAG categories obtained Recall@10 values of 90.0% for cognates, 77.2% for cross-language retrieval, 100% for historical retrieval, and 70.0% for morphology. Table 7 reports a category mean of 84.30%, a minimum of 70.00%, a maximum of 100%, and a population standard deviation of 11.55. Historical retrieval again benefits from explicit lineage mappings. Morphology is the lowest category, while cross-language retrieval is higher than in the semantic-search benchmark but remains below cognate and historical retrieval.

The average stored retrieval latency was 53.173 ms. This is an observed value from the project environment, whose exact hardware was not recorded. It cannot be treated as a portable systems benchmark or directly compared with results obtained on other machines. Cold-start and warm-model latency are also not separated in the stored statistic.

Table 6 and the source-contribution data show that successful results can contain multiple evidence traces. Semantic and morphology traces each occurred in 915 successful hits, dictionary traces in 798, and cognate and historical traces in 675 each. These values overlap because one retrieved document may carry several source types. They demonstrate multi-source participation but do not establish independent causal contributions. The retriever also uses manually fixed component weights, and those weights have not been tuned on an independent validation set.

## 6.6 QA Evaluation

The retrieval-based QA service achieved answer accuracy of 83.80%, source accuracy of 99.70%, and top-k support coverage of 83.80% on 1,000 questions. Table 5 reports derived Wilson intervals of 81.39–85.95% for answer accuracy and support coverage, and 99.12–99.90% for source accuracy. The equality of answer accuracy and support coverage follows from the stored evaluation: both require overlap between expected words and returned answer or support items.

Category-level answer accuracy was 89.2% for cognate questions, 77.2% for cross-language questions, 100% for historical questions, and 68.8% for morphology questions. Source accuracy remained between 99.2% and 100% across categories. Table 7 reports a category mean answer accuracy of 83.80%, a minimum of 68.80%, a maximum of 100%, and a population standard deviation of 11.83.

The gap between source accuracy and answer accuracy is informative. The system usually routed evidence through the expected source type, yet it did not always return an acceptable expected word. Source tracing is therefore more reliable in this benchmark than term-level answer selection. However, source accuracy checks internal source categories and identifiers, not bibliographic citation correctness. Likewise, answer accuracy measures expected-word overlap in a structured template response; it does not assess completeness, explanation quality, grammaticality, or independent factual correctness.

## 6.7 Discussion

The strongest repository-supported finding is that the pipeline retrieves many expected related forms within the first ten candidates. Embedding Top-10 was 86.06%, semantic-search Recall@10 was 82.30%, RAG Recall@10 was 84.30%, and QA answer accuracy was 83.80%. Figure 3 shows the consistency of these upper-cutoff results across the pipeline. Explicit metadata paths are particularly effective for historical retrieval, while morphology produces positive cluster separation and broad feature availability.

At the same time, first-rank performance is materially lower. Embedding Top-1 was 40.80%, semantic Recall@1 was 36.25%, and RAG Recall@1 was 27.00%. The model and retrieval stack are therefore better suited to candidate generation and evidence collection than to unqualified single-answer selection. For practical Turkic NLP applications, reranking, expert filtering, or stronger disambiguation would be needed when only one result can be shown.

Cross-language ranking and morphology QA remain the clearest weaknesses. Cross-language semantic Recall@10 was 54.57%, and morphology QA accuracy was 68.8%. Nearest-neighbor audits show that highly similar inflectional and generated variants can dominate neighborhoods. This reflects both FastText’s sensitivity to character overlap and the composition of the morphology-oriented dataset. More diverse semantic contexts, independently curated translation-equivalent data, and controlled hard-negative construction would be required to distinguish orthographic, morphological, semantic, and etymological similarity more reliably.

The evaluation design is the principal limitation. Semantic-search, RAG, and QA benchmarks are generated from the same semantic index against which they are evaluated, and the embedding benchmark draws from repository cognate and embedding resources. Several datasets contain synthetic, projected, or candidate-review material. The results are therefore internally reproducible engineering measurements, not independent estimates of linguistic generalization. The derived intervals in Table 5 describe uncertainty within these benchmarks and do not remove the underlying leakage or provenance concerns.

Further limitations include the absence of a reported baseline, the absence of controlled retraining ablations, incomplete provenance, uneven normalization across languages, and the lack of expert-reviewed cognate and historical gold standards. Table 6 must remain a proxy contribution analysis, and Table 7 is a derived descriptive summary rather than a statistical test. No claim of superiority over standard FastText, transformer embeddings, BM25, or other multilingual systems can be supported from the current artifacts.

For Turkic NLP, the results nevertheless illustrate a useful engineering direction: morphology, cognate groups, and historical lineage can be represented in one searchable resource and retained as inspectable evidence paths. The architecture supports multiple scripts and combines modern and historical varieties without requiring a separate model for each language. The next evidential step is not stronger interpretation of the current scores, but independent expert-reviewed evaluation, source-separated train and test data, standard baselines, and controlled feature-removal experiments.
